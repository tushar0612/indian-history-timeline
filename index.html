Indian History: Kingdoms & Rulers, Oldest to Newest

A chronological map of the major Indian dynasties from the Indus Valley Civilization to Independence — with a timeline showing which kingdoms overlapped, followed by details on key rulers and what each kingdom is remembered for.

Quick correction on the starting point: Chandragupta I was not the first Indian ruler — he founded the Gupta Empire around 320 CE, which is actually quite late in this story (roughly two-thirds of the way through). You may be thinking of Chandragupta Maurya, a completely different person nearly 650 years earlier, who founded the Maurya Empire in 322 BCE and is usually taught as the first ruler to unify most of India under one crown. Before him there were centuries of the Vedic period and, before that, the Indus Valley Civilization — which had cities and trade but no named rulers we know of (its script is still undeciphered). This page starts at the very beginning and marks both Chandraguptas clearly so you never mix them up again.

    1. Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500 – 1900 BCE)

    No known named rulers — the script is still undeciphered. Not a kingdom in the usual sense; a network of highly organized cities.

    Mohenjo-daro ruins · Wikimedia Commons

    Harappa & Mohenjo-daro

    c. 2500 – 1900 BCE · Indus & Ghaggar-Hakra river basins (modern Pakistan/NW India)
    Governance

    No temples-to-kings or palace complexes have been found, so historians debate whether it was ruled by kings, councils, or merchant elites — unusually egalitarian for its time.

    • Precise urban planning: grid-pattern streets, standardized brick sizes
    • The world's earliest known covered drainage/sewage systems
    • Standardized weights and measures used across a huge trade network
    • Long-distance trade with Mesopotamia (seals found in Sumer)
    • Undeciphered Indus script — the civilization's biggest remaining mystery

    2. Vedic Period (c. 1500 – 600 BCE)

    Indo-Aryan tribes settle the Gangetic plain; the Vedas are composed; tribal chiefdoms slowly become territorial kingdoms (Janapadas).

    Kuru Kingdom

    c. 1200 – 800 BCE · near modern Delhi/Haryana
    • King Parikshit — a semi-legendary king credited with consolidating the Kuru realm; grandson of the Pandavas of the Mahabharata tradition.
    Famous for
    • Setting: the Kurukshetra War of the Mahabharata is traditionally dated to this era
    • Compilation and ritual codification of the Vedas

    Videha & Kosala (Later Vedic)

    c. 1000 – 600 BCE · modern Bihar/UP border and Ayodhya region
    • King Janaka of Videha — philosopher-king famed for hosting great sages; father of Sita in the Ramayana tradition.
    Famous for
    • Rise of philosophical debate that fed into the Upanishads
    • Setting for much of the Ramayana narrative

    3. The Sixteen Mahajanapadas (c. 600 – 345 BCE)

    Sixteen major kingdoms/republics covered the Gangetic plain at once — this is the era where "so many kingdoms at the same time" really starts. Magadha slowly out-competes and absorbs the rest.

    Magadha (the eventual winner)

    c. 600 – 345 BCE · capital Rajgir/Girivraja, later Pataliputra · modern Bihar
    • Bimbisara — expanded Magadha through strategic marriages and alliances rather than pure conquest; contemporary of both the Buddha and Mahavira.
    • Ajatashatru — his son; a more aggressive conqueror who annexed Vaishali (a powerful neighboring republic) and fortified Pataliputra, which became India's greatest capital for the next thousand years.
    Famous for
    • Becoming the springboard for the Nanda and then Maurya Empires
    • Hosting the lifetimes of the Buddha and Mahavira

    Kosala

    c. 600 – 490 BCE · capital Shravasti · modern eastern UP
    • Prasenajit — Magadha's main rival for decades; eventually his kingdom was absorbed by Magadha via a marriage-alliance-turned-annexation.
    Famous for
    • One of the largest and most powerful of the 16 Mahajanapadas before losing out to Magadha

    Avanti

    c. 600 – 400 BCE · capital Ujjain · modern Malwa, MP
    • Pradyota — controlled key trade routes between north and south India; his dynasty was a serious military rival to Magadha for a time.
    Famous for
    • Ujjain's long career afterward as a major center of astronomy and Sanskrit learning

    Vatsa

    c. 600 – 450 BCE · capital Kaushambi · modern UP near Allahabad
    • Udayana — a romantic, semi-legendary king whose exploits became a favorite subject of later Sanskrit drama.
    Famous for
    • Kaushambi as a major trading and pilgrimage city for centuries after
    Also happening right now: Gautama Buddha (founder of Buddhism, c. 563 – 483 BCE, dates debated) and Mahavira (founder of Jainism, c. 599 – 527 BCE, dates debated) both lived during this exact period, traveling between these very kingdoms — arguably the most consequential few decades in Indian religious history. Buddha was born in the Shakya republic (in Kosala's orbit), attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya in Magadha, and spent most of his teaching life moving between Magadha (patron: King Bimbisara, then Ajatashatru) and Kosala (patron: King Prasenajit), before dying at Kushinagar in the Malla republic. Mahavira was born near Vaishali in the Vajji/Licchavi confederacy (later annexed by Magadha) and attained his final liberation (nirvana) at Pavapuri, within Magadha.

    4. Nanda Dynasty (c. 345 – 322 BCE)

    Nanda Empire

    c. 345 – 322 BCE · capital Pataliputra
    • Mahapadma Nanda — first ruler to unify nearly all of northern India under one throne, from Punjab to Bengal; built a famously massive army but taxed the population heavily and was deeply unpopular.
    Famous for
    • Being the first true "empire" (not just a regional kingdom) in Indian history
    • Its unpopularity, which gave a young Chandragupta Maurya his opening

    5. Maurya Empire (322 – 185 BCE)

    The first empire to cover almost the entire Indian subcontinent, plus parts of Afghanistan. This is Chandragupta Maurya, not Chandragupta I of the Guptas.

    The Lion Capital of Ashoka, Sarnath (now India's national emblem) · Wikimedia Commons

    Chandragupta Maurya

    r. c. 322 – 297 BCE

    Overthrew the hated Nanda dynasty with the guidance of his strategist and mentor Chanakya (Kautilya), author of the Arthashastra — one of the world's earliest treatises on statecraft. He then fought Alexander's former general Seleucus I, won, and in the peace treaty received large territories in the northwest plus a Greek princess in marriage.

    Famous for
    • Founding the first pan-Indian empire; later abdicating to become a Jain monk

    Bindusara

    r. c. 297 – 273 BCE

    Chandragupta's son; pushed the empire's borders deep into the Deccan (southern plateau), earning the Greek nickname "Amitrochates" ("slayer of enemies"). Kept diplomatic ties with the Hellenistic kings to the west.

    Ashoka the Great

    r. c. 268 – 232 BCE

    The empire's most famous ruler. Conquered Kalinga (modern Odisha) in a war so bloody that he renounced further conquest, converted to Buddhism, and spent the rest of his reign governing through dhamma (moral law) — carved onto rock edicts and pillars across the subcontinent, several of which survive today (the Ashoka Chakra on India's flag comes from his Sarnath pillar). Sent Buddhist missionaries as far as Sri Lanka and possibly the Mediterranean.

    Famous for
    • Turning Buddhism from a regional sect into a world religion
    • Some of the earliest surviving royal inscriptions in India

    6. Post-Mauryan Fragmentation (c. 185 BCE – 320 CE)

    The empire splits. This is the messiest "everyone at once" period before Gupta: a north-Indian successor state, waves of Central Asian invaders in the northwest, a long-lived Deccan power, and independent Tamil kingdoms in the far south — all running in parallel for centuries.

    The Great Stupa at Sanchi, expanded under the Shungas · Wikimedia Commons

    Shunga Dynasty

    185 – 73 BCE · north/central India
    • Pushyamitra Shunga — a Mauryan general who seized the throne; sponsored a Sanskrit/Brahmanical revival and major additions to the Sanchi Stupa.

    Indo-Greek Kingdom

    c. 180 – 10 BCE · Punjab/Afghanistan (leftover Greek settlers from Alexander's campaigns)
    • Menander I ("Milinda") — the most famous Indo-Greek king; reportedly converted to Buddhism and is the subject of a Buddhist text, the Milindapanha.
    Famous for
    • Greco-Buddhist art — the earliest Buddha statues, sculpted in Greek style

    Indo-Scythians (Sakas)

    c. 100 BCE – 400 CE · western India (Gujarat, Malwa)

    Central Asian nomadic warriors who carved out kingdoms in western India after being pushed out of their homeland; the "Saka Era" calendar (still used in India's official calendar) is named after them.

    Kushan Empire

    c. 30 – 375 CE · Central Asia to North India
    • Kanishka the Great — controlled a huge stretch of the Silk Road; convened the Fourth Buddhist Council; his patronage produced Gandhara art (another Greek-influenced Buddhist art style).
    Famous for
    • Connecting India directly into Silk Road trade with China, Persia, and Rome

    Satavahana Dynasty

    c. 230 BCE – 220 CE · the Deccan (modern Maharashtra/Telangana/AP)
    • Gautamiputra Satakarni — the dynasty's greatest king; drove out Saka invaders from parts of the Deccan and controlled lucrative Indian Ocean trade routes.
    Famous for
    • Longest-running dynasty of this era (nearly 450 years); patronized the Amaravati Buddhist art style

    Sangam-Age Tamil Kingdoms: Chera, Chola, Pandya

    c. 300 BCE – 300 CE · far south (Kerala/Tamil Nadu)

    Three simultaneous Tamil kingdoms, constantly at war with each other, who nonetheless produced the Sangam literature — some of the oldest surviving Tamil poetry — and ran an active spice trade with Rome (Roman coins are still found on the Kerala coast).

    7. Gupta Empire (c. 320 – 550 CE)

    India's "Golden Age." This is where Chandragupta I actually belongs — nearly 650 years after Chandragupta Maurya.

    The rust-resistant Iron Pillar of Delhi, erected under Chandragupta II · Wikimedia Commons

    Chandragupta I

    r. c. 320 – 335 CE

    Founder of the Gupta dynasty proper; expanded a modest regional holding into a real kingdom largely through a strategic marriage to a princess of the powerful Lichhavi clan, gaining territory and legitimacy at once.

    Samudragupta

    r. c. 335 – 375 CE

    Sometimes called the "Napoleon of India" for near-constant, near-unbroken military campaigns that brought most of northern India and parts of the south under Gupta influence — yet he was also a poet and accomplished musician, and his coins depict him playing the veena.

    Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya)

    r. c. 375 – 415 CE

    Reign marks the empire's peak. His court hosted the legendary "Navaratna" (Nine Gems), including the poet-playwright Kalidasa. This period saw the mathematician-astronomer Aryabhata lay groundwork used across the Gupta era, refinement of the decimal number system with a symbol for zero, and major advances in medicine and metallurgy (the rust-resistant Iron Pillar of Delhi dates to this time).

    Famous for
    • Peak Gupta territory, art, literature, science — the reference point for "Golden Age of India"
    At the same time: the Vakataka dynasty ruled the Deccan and intermarried with the Guptas; further south the Sangam-age Tamil kingdoms had recently faded, giving way to early Pallava power. The Gupta Empire eventually weakened under repeated invasions by the Hunas (White Huns) from Central Asia.

    8. Classical Regional Kingdoms (c. 550 – 1200 CE)

    No single empire controls India for 650 years — instead, three-to-five major powers run in parallel and constantly fight over the same territory. This is the densest "everyone at once" era on the whole timeline.

    Kailasa Temple, Ellora — carved downward from a single rock face by the Rashtrakutas · Wikimedia Commons

    Vardhana Dynasty (Harsha)

    606 – 647 CE · north India, capital Kannauj
    • Harshavardhana — briefly reunified much of the north; hosted the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, whose travel diary is a key historical source; patronized the great Nalanda university.

    Chalukyas of Badami

    543 – 753 CE · Deccan
    • Pulakeshin II — famously handed Harsha a rare defeat when Harsha tried to expand south, keeping the Deccan independent of northern control.

    Pallava Dynasty

    c. 550 – 897 CE (peak) · Tamil country
    • Narasimhavarman I — decisively defeated the Chalukyas; built the rock-cut monuments at Mahabalipuram, including the Shore Temple.

    Rashtrakuta Dynasty

    753 – 982 CE · Deccan (succeeded the Chalukyas)
    • Krishna I — commissioned the Kailasa Temple at Ellora, an entire temple carved downward out of a single rock face — still considered an engineering marvel.

    Pratihara (Gurjara-Pratihara) Dynasty

    c. 730 – 1036 CE · north/west India
    • Mihira Bhoja — brought the empire to its territorial peak and fought the decades-long Tripartite Struggle against the Palas and Rashtrakutas over Kannauj. (Earlier Pratihara rulers, like Nagabhata I, had already halted Arab expansion out of Sindh back in the 8th century.)

    Pala Dynasty

    750 – 1161 CE · Bengal & Bihar
    • Dharmapala — the dynasty's most powerful ruler; a major patron of Buddhism who founded Vikramashila, one of medieval India's great universities.

    Western Chalukyas (Kalyani)

    973 – 1189 CE · Deccan/Karnataka, capital Kalyani
    • Vikramaditya VI — succeeded the Rashtrakutas as the dominant Deccan power; his 50-year reign was stable and prosperous enough that Karnataka used a "Chalukya-Vikrama era" calendar dated from his coronation for centuries afterward.
    Famous for
    • One of the longest single reigns in Indian history (1076–1126); constant wars with the Cholas over Vengi (coastal Andhra)

    Chola Empire (Imperial phase)

    848 – 1279 CE · Tamil country
    • Raja Raja Chola I — built the massive Brihadeeswarar Temple at Thanjavur and conquered Sri Lanka.
    • Rajendra Chola I — his son; sent a naval expedition across the Bay of Bengal against the Srivijaya empire (modern Indonesia/Malaysia) — one of very few overseas military campaigns in Indian history.
    Famous for
    • The most powerful navy in Indian history; sophisticated local self-government via village councils (documented in detailed inscriptions)

    Hoysala Dynasty

    1026 – 1343 CE · Karnataka

    Rose as the Chalukyas/Rashtrakutas declined in the Deccan; famous almost entirely for exceptionally intricate temple architecture, especially at Belur and Halebidu.

    Memory hook — the "Tripartite Struggle": for roughly two centuries, the Pratiharas (northwest), Palas (east), and Rashtrakutas (Deccan) fought repeatedly over control of Kannauj, the symbolic seat of northern power — a three-way tug-of-war that no single side ever won outright.

    9. Delhi Sultanate & Vijayanagara/Bahmani (1206 – 1526 CE)

    The first sustained Muslim rule in north India, running for over three centuries through several dynasties — while the south splits into a Hindu empire and a rival Muslim sultanate that fought each other constantly.

    Delhi Sultanate

    1206 – 1526 CE · five successive dynasties
    • Qutb-ud-din Aibak (Slave/Mamluk dynasty, 1206–1290) — a former slave-general who founded the Sultanate; began construction of the Qutub Minar.
    • Alauddin Khalji (Khalji dynasty, 1290–1320) — repelled repeated Mongol invasions and imposed strict market price controls; pushed Sultanate armies deep into the Deccan for the first time.
    • Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Tughlaq dynasty, 1320–1414) — infamous for forcibly relocating the entire capital from Delhi to Daulatabad and back, and for an experimental (and failed) token currency.
    • Ibrahim Lodi (Lodi dynasty, 1451–1526) — the last Delhi Sultan, defeated and killed by Babur at the First Battle of Panipat, ending the Sultanate and opening the Mughal era.

    Vijayanagara Empire

    1336 – 1646 CE · south India
    • Krishnadevaraya — the empire's peak ruler; a major patron of Telugu and Kannada literature, oversaw huge temple-building projects at the capital, Hampi, and repeatedly defeated the neighboring Deccan sultanates.
    Famous for
    • Being the last great Hindu empire before the Mughal era fully dominates the subcontinent

    Bahmani Sultanate

    1347 – 1527 CE · Deccan

    Broke away from the Delhi Sultanate to rule the Deccan independently; constant rival of Vijayanagara. Eventually fractured into five smaller Deccan Sultanates (including Bijapur and Golconda), which lasted into the Mughal period.

    10. Mughal Empire & Contemporaries (1526 – 1857 CE)

    The last great pre-colonial empire — and, at the same time, the rise of the powers that would eventually dismantle it from within: the Marathas, the Sikhs, and the British.

    Mughal Empire

    1526 – 1857 CE
    • Babur — a Central Asian prince descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan; founded the empire by defeating the Delhi Sultanate at Panipat in 1526.
    • Akbar — the true architect of the empire; expanded it hugely, built alliances with Rajput kings through marriage, and promoted religious tolerance through his own syncretic court philosophy, Din-i-Ilahi.
    • Shah Jahan — presided over the empire's cultural peak; commissioned the Taj Mahal in memory of his wife, Mumtaz Mahal.
    • Aurangzeb — brought the empire to its largest-ever territorial extent, but his stricter religious policies and near-constant wars (especially against the rising Marathas) badly strained imperial finances and set up its long decline.
    Famous for
    • The Taj Mahal, Red Fort, and a highly centralized administrative system later partly reused by the British

    Maratha Empire

    1674 – 1818 CE · Deccan, later most of India
    • Shivaji Maharaj — founder; built a formidable hill-fort network and used fast guerrilla tactics to carve out an independent kingdom from Mughal and Bijapur territory; later Peshwas (prime ministers) expanded Maratha influence across most of the subcontinent by the mid-1700s.
    Famous for
    • Becoming, by the 18th century, arguably the strongest single power in India — before losing decisively to the British at the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1818)

    Sikh Empire

    1799 – 1849 CE · Punjab
    • Maharaja Ranjit Singh — unified the previously fractured Sikh misls (confederacies) into a single, well-organized state with a modernized army; the last major independent Indian kingdom to fall to the British.

    11. British Colonial Period (1757 – 1947 CE)

    East India Company Rule

    1757 – 1858 CE

    Robert Clive's victory at the Battle of Plassey in 1757 gave the Company control of Bengal, and it expanded, region by region, over the next century — a trading company, not a government, effectively ruling most of India.

    Revolt of 1857

    1857 – 1858 CE

    A large-scale uprising against Company rule, led in part by Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi and nominally supported by Bahadur Shah II, the last Mughal emperor. Suppressed, but it ended Company rule — the British Crown took direct control afterward.

    British Raj & the Freedom Movement

    1858 – 1947 CE
    • Indian National Congress (founded 1885) — became the main vehicle for the independence movement.
    • Mohandas Gandhi — led mass non-violent campaigns (Non-Cooperation, the Salt March, Quit India) that made independence politically unsustainable for Britain.
    • Subhas Chandra Bose — pursued an armed alternative, leading the Indian National Army against the British during WWII.
    • Muhammad Ali Jinnah — led the Muslim League's push for a separate Muslim-majority state, resulting in Partition alongside independence.

    12. Independence (1947)

    1947 — Independence & Partition

    British India was partitioned into India and Pakistan on 15 August 1947. Jawaharlal Nehru became independent India's first Prime Minister. This closes roughly 4,400 years of the story above — from unnamed Indus Valley city-planners to a modern nation-state.
